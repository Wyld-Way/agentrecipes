## The problem

You want to grow — more users, more traffic, a new audience — so you throw
resources at a promising idea: a new site, a content push, a partnership
effort. Weeks or months later you're still investing in it, nobody set a
date to check whether it's actually working, and you can't tell if it's a
real channel or a pet project everyone's now afraid to kill. Meanwhile the
one thing that DOES work has no dedicated owner, so it runs on whoever
remembers to touch it and quietly decays from neglect.

## What you get

A container for running many small growth experiments ("seeds") at once,
without any single one draining the org and without a good one dying from
neglect:

- **Every bet is cheap to kill before you plant it** — the metric and the
  date you'll check are written down at the start, never invented after the
  fact once someone's attached to the outcome.
- **A named loop-back keeps every bet honest** — a seed exists to feed the
  core product users, attention, or signal, or it's a hobby and gets killed
  as one.
- **On-site and off-site growth work run as two clearly separated jobs** —
  ranking the surface and routing its traffic vs. building resources and
  earning placements outside it — so neither gets muddled into a single
  unaccountable role.
- **The moment a bet actually works, it gets a dedicated owner** and stops
  competing for portfolio attention with the next ten experiments.
- **A standing worked example** to calibrate how real "the smallest real
  version" should actually be.

## Use when / skip when

**Use when** you (or your growth function) run more than one speculative
growth idea at a time and need a way to judge a portfolio rather than argue
about one idea in isolation — new content sites, audience or community
plays, SEO/content surfaces, small tools meant to feed a top-of-funnel.

**Skip when** you have exactly one growth initiative and it's already a
committed, funded product bet, not an experiment — this recipe is about
running many cheap experiments safely, not executing a single approved plan
(route that through your normal product process instead). Skip the off-site
outreach half entirely if you have no real bandwidth for it — a half-run
outreach loop that goes stale and generic is worse than never starting one.

## Ingredients

- A written portfolio/bench doc listing every seed, its bet, its loop-back,
  and its kill signal — visible in one place, not scattered across chat.
- Someone (a person or a role) who owns the portfolio and decides what to
  plant or kill, distinct from whoever executes any single seed.
- Two standing execution roles once a seed is live: one for on-site work
  (content, on-page/technical SEO, testing the promotion-to-core placement)
  and one for off-site work (building genuinely link-worthy resources,
  finding real placements, drafting outreach — a human still sends every
  message).
- Basic traffic/conversion analytics on whatever surface the seed lives on.
- A standing rule for what happens the moment a seed actually works.

## The recipe

**1. Before you plant anything, write the kill signal down — the metric AND
the date.** A seed with no defined kill signal is not planted yet; it's a
hobby you haven't admitted to. Pick a real threshold (a sessions, signups,
or engagement number) and a real date (30–90 days out is typical) BEFORE you
invest a single hour, while you have no attachment to the outcome. Writing
it down after the seed exists is how sunk cost wins the argument later.

**2. Ship the smallest version that is genuinely real, not a roadmap.**
"Smallest" cuts scope, never quality — one page that actually ranks and
actually captures the loop-back action beats a five-page plan for a
polished site nobody built. If what you'd ship doesn't feel like "that's
actually live," it's still a plan with homework attached.

**3. State the loop-back explicitly, in one sentence, before you ship.** Name
exactly how this seed is meant to feed the thing you actually monetize —
more signups, more attention, a specific new audience, direct
cross-promotion. A clever project with no stated loop-back is a hobby
wearing a growth costume; kill it as one, however fun it is to run.

**4. Separate "get it built and ranking" from "get it found in the world" as
two standing jobs, not one blurred role.** One job lives ON the surface:
on-page and technical mechanics, evergreen content targeting real search
intent, and — the job most teams skip — continuously testing and sharpening
the placement and copy of the promotion back to the core product; a page
that ranks but converts nobody is only half a job done. The other job lives
OFF the surface: building resources genuinely worth citing, finding
legitimate places (directories, communities, partner sites) the seed
belongs, and drafting real, personal, specific outreach — never templated
blasts, never a placement that breaks a community's own rules. A human
sends every outreach message; the drafting role never sends on its own.

**5. Judge the portfolio, not any single bet, and expect most seeds to
die.** If every seed you plant survives, you aren't swinging big enough on
any of them — a portfolio where one seed pays off big covers many that
didn't. Review the whole bench together on a cadence, not seed-by-seed
whenever someone happens to remember.

**6. Read the soil before inventing new ground.** Before spinning up
something net-new, check what you already own that's sitting dormant — a
parked domain, a half-built tool, an audience you already touch but don't
convert. Reviving something you already paid for beats a greenfield bet
almost every time.

**7. When a seed hits its kill signal, kill it — without ceremony and
without re-litigating the bet after the fact.** A seed that "sort of" hit
its number and gets a quiet extension is exactly how a portfolio turns into
an unkillable graveyard of pets.

**8. When a seed clears its bar and takes root, hand it to a dedicated
owner — don't let the portfolio-holder keep running it part-time.** The
moment a seed is a real surface with a real number, not a bet anymore, it
needs someone whose whole job is that one seed's surface, loop, and number.
The portfolio-holder plants and holds the BENCH, never a graduated seed —
running a winner part-time alongside ten new bets is how winners quietly
rot from neglect, the same failure as never watering them at all.

**9. Write down the honest lesson every seed teaches, win or lose, before
you move on.** The single most valuable output of a killed or graduated
seed is usually not the traffic number — it's the reusable insight about
what does or doesn't make a viable seed (for example: "we can't
out-content a search engine that already indexes the same public data — a
seed needs a reason to exist beyond being a nicer version of something that
already exists for free"). Losing this lesson is losing the only
compounding value a dead seed produced.

## What might go wrong

- **The undead seed.** Bites when a seed's kill-signal date arrives and the
  number is close-but-not-quite, so someone extends "just one more cycle"
  without a new number or date attached. Symptom: a bench that only grows,
  never composts, and a portfolio-holder who can no longer tell a real bet
  from a pet. Prevention: the kill signal is a contract set before
  attachment forms; changing it after the fact requires the same rigor as
  setting a new one — a new number, a new date, written down.
- **The ownerless winner.** Bites the moment a seed clears its bar and the
  portfolio-holder keeps running it "for now" alongside the next round of
  new bets. Symptom: a proven channel slowly stops getting attention as
  newer, shinier experiments crowd it out, and it decays from neglect
  exactly like an unwatered failure would. Prevention: graduation triggers a
  dedicated-owner handoff in the same cycle, not eventually.
- **The content-only seed with no loop-back.** Bites when a seed is
  genuinely well-executed — real traffic, real rankings — but nobody ever
  wired a clear, tested path back to the core product, so all that earned
  attention evaporates at the seed's own edge. Symptom: real traffic
  numbers that never move the metric that actually matters. Prevention:
  state and test the loop-back from day one (step 3), and treat "ranks but
  doesn't route" as an unfinished job, not a win.
- **Competing with a platform you can't beat.** Bites when a seed's core
  value proposition is a nicer version of something a dominant, free,
  general-purpose competitor already does at scale using the same public
  data. Symptom: real but shallow traffic that never converts, because the
  seed never had a reason to exist beyond looking nicer. Prevention: name
  the seed's DEFENSIBLE reason to exist — a friction removed, an audience
  only you can reach, a promise a generic competitor structurally can't
  make — before shipping, not after the traffic disappoints.
- **Off-site growth that burns the brand.** Bites when outreach or
  placement work is templated, sent at volume, or pushed into communities
  that didn't ask for it — cheap wins now, a reputation cost that outlasts
  the seed. Prevention: every outreach is personal and specific, a human
  always sends, and a community or directory's own self-promotion rules are
  read before anything is proposed there, not after a complaint.
- **Blurring rank-work and route-work into one blob.** Bites when the
  on-site/off-site split doesn't exist and one person tries to hold "did it
  rank," "does it convert," and "did we get the backlink" all at once —
  something always gets dropped, usually the harder-to-see conversion work,
  because ranking is easier to measure and crowds it out. Prevention: keep
  the two jobs distinct even if one person does both, with a separate
  output from each every cycle.

## The pulse

- The bench doc is read live, not memorized, at the start of every session
  touching growth work — it is the single source of truth for what's
  planted, watering, graduated, or composted, and whoever executes any
  single seed reads it first.
- A standing cadence review of the WHOLE portfolio, not seed-by-seed ad hoc,
  is where kill signals actually get enforced — a kill signal nobody
  reviews on schedule is a kill signal that never fires.
- The two execution roles are continuous, not one-shot: each cycle ends
  with a written "next" marker for its own future run, so momentum survives
  a handoff between sessions.
- A composted-seeds section stays in the bench doc, even when empty, as a
  visible admission that killing is normal and expected — not a section
  anyone is tempted to quietly delete out of embarrassment.
